# Prose Style Guide

STATUS: DRAFT — Gate 1 level, directly formalizing the source brief's
prose requirements; will be stress-tested against real drafted pages
once Gate 5 closes and drafting begins, and amended via `DECISION_LOG.md`
if a rule needs to change after sample chapters set precedent.

## POV and mechanics

- Close third person, past tense. ~85–95% Mara Voss.
- The remaining ~5–15% may include interview transcripts, claim notes,
  letters, building records, edited-footage transcripts, continuity
  files, and brief Elena fragments — only where a specific chapter's
  brief justifies it, never as a default crutch for exposition. No
  chapter should lean on a document insert to avoid dramatizing a scene
  that should be dramatized.
- No head-hopping within a Mara-POV scene. See
  `narrative_distance.md` for the full mechanics of what "close third"
  permits (inference, reading someone's behavior) versus what it
  doesn't (accessing another character's actual interiority as narrated
  fact).
- Native-level American English throughout — spelling, idiom, and
  register should read as unmistakably American (this book is NOT
  written in British English conventions).

## Prose goals

Clear. Specific. Controlled. Emotionally intelligent. Atmospheric
through concrete detail, not mood adjectives. Elegant without showing
off. Commercially readable. Psychologically deep.

## Banned — do not use these, full stop

- Generic cinematic prose (writing that reads like a camera direction
  rather than an inhabited perspective).
- Melodrama.
- Excessive sentence fragments as a tic.
- Excessive one-line paragraphs as a tic.
- Excessive em dashes.
- Repeated heartbeat / breathing / cold / darkness / silence / nameless-
  dread imagery — these are the genre's most over-used atmosphere
  shortcuts; dread in this book comes from specific, concrete
  observation (a records discrepancy, an object out of place, a date
  that doesn't fit), never from generic sensory atmosphere.
- "She let out a breath she did not know she was holding" and any close
  variant.
- Repetitive "the kind of" phrasing.
- Vague ominous language (something felt wrong; a chill; a sense of
  dread) unsupported by a specific cause.
- Emotional over-explanation — state what happened and let the emotional
  weight land; don't append the emotional label to it.
- Therapy-language exposition (characters narrating their own
  psychology in clinical terms — "I have trust issues because of my
  attachment style" — is banned regardless of how true it might be;
  psychological insight belongs to the narration's precision, not to
  characters diagnosing themselves out loud).
- Everyone speaking in aphorisms (no character, including minor ones,
  gets a constant stream of quotable one-liners — reserve genuine
  aphoristic lines for rare, earned moments).
- Cheap cliffhangers (a chapter ending that manufactures tension by
  withholding a fact the POV character already possesses, purely to
  create a false question).
- **Withholding information Mara naturally knows only to trick the
  reader** — this is the single most important prose-level fair-play
  rule in the whole project (see `02_novel_bible/genre_contract.md`). If
  Mara has concluded something, the reader gets to know she's concluded
  it, even if the conclusion itself is later proven wrong. Tension comes
  from what Mara doesn't yet know, never from an artificial gap between
  what she knows and what the narration admits to the reader.
- A full, project-specific banned list (including phrases and beats
  specifically adjacent to the rejected prior branch's executed style)
  lives in `banned_cliches.md` — check both files.

## Dialogue

See `dialogue_guide.md` for full treatment. Summary requirement: every
line of dialogue must serve a goal, carry subtext, sound distinct to its
speaker, allow for natural misunderstanding, and use contractions the
way real speech does. No exposition dumps disguised as conversation.

## Sentence-level craft

- Vary sentence length deliberately — a page of similar-length sentences
  reads as flat regardless of individual sentence quality.
- Cut filtering words (she noticed, she felt, she saw, she realized)
  except where the act of noticing/realizing is itself the point of the
  sentence.
- No two consecutive paragraphs should open with the same subject or
  grammatical structure.
- Precision over volume: one exact detail beats three approximate ones.

## Chapter discipline (once drafting begins)

- Open in scene, not in reflection, weather, or throat-clearing.
- Close on forward pressure (a question, an unresolved choice, a new
  piece of information that changes the stakes) — never a flat summary
  of what just happened, and never a manufactured cliffhanger of the
  banned kind above.
