## Part I — The Vacant Apartment (Ch. 1–12, ~22,000–25,000 words)

The novel opens on Mara Voss mid-competence — precise, controlled,
good at her job — receiving and half-listening to a voicemail from her
younger sister Elena that she recognizes, from long habit, as "an Elena
thing" and sets aside (selected Opening, Option 2 —
`opening_options.md`). Within the same or following chapter, Mara learns
Elena hasn't been seen in longer than fits even Elena's pattern, and
finally plays the full voicemail — fragmented, urgent, impossible to
explain away.

Mara's professional access gets her further, faster, than an ordinary
family member — and immediately into friction with NYPD Detective Teo
Marchetti, who is running the actual missing-persons case and is not
interested in a civilian, however credentialed, working his
investigation informally. Their relationship starts adversarial and
begins, slowly, to shift as Mara demonstrates she's finding real things
his process isn't built to surface.

Elena's last confirmed address is Apartment 4C, Bellwether House — a
six-story Brooklyn walk-up whose management records list the unit as
vacant for twelve years. Mara begins interviewing residents and hits the
book's central strangeness directly: multiple people recall a woman
matching Elena's description, but as different people entirely.

- **Evelyn Price**, a retired nurse, remembers "Lena Hart" — a pregnant
  woman fleeing an abusive partner, complete with prenatal records and a
  handmade blanket.
- **Jonah Bell**, a family-law attorney, remembers "Dana Kessler" — a
  legal researcher who helped his clients disappear from coercive
  marriages.
- **Irena Petrov**, the building's oldest resident, remembers "Anna" —
  but from roughly twenty years earlier, a detail Mara initially
  discounts as an old woman's confusion.
- **Malik Reed**, thirteen, has glimpsed a woman moving through the
  building's disused service stairwell and dumbwaiter-adjacent routes —
  dismissed by every adult he's mentioned it to.
- **Rina Cho**, a documentary editor, has research footage of a woman
  helping with a film about people erased from public records.
- **Calvin Moss**, a data broker working almost entirely remotely, knew
  a multilingual records specialist under yet another name.
- **Gabriel Knox**, the superintendent, claims to remember nothing —
  and his analog maintenance logs don't match his claim.

Mara's own read on the pattern (real evidence, no lies, no possible
single ordinary life) starts to point toward something organized rather
than coincidental. Part I closes on its climax: during an authorized
inspection of Apartment 4C, Mara finds a concealed cache of identity
documents, all bearing Elena's face, none sharing a name — and an
envelope addressed specifically to her. Inside: "You were the first
tenant."

## Part II — Seven Lives (Ch. 13–34, ~43,000–48,000 words)

Part II is the investigation's expansion and the book's emotional
excavation, run in parallel per
`02_novel_bible/thematic_argument.md`'s protection clause — every
procedural advance paired with real movement in Mara's understanding of
her own history and her relationship with Elena.

Mara traces the Palinode Continuity Group and begins to understand its
scope: not simple forgery, but manufactured continuity — real witnessed
relationships built around invented people, sustained sometimes for
years or decades. She confirms (working toward, not yet fully locking
until Gate 3) that Apartment 4C's "vacancy" is a specific paperwork
mechanism — a shell entity, a lapsed synthetic-tenant lease exactly
twelve years old — rather than a simple absence of record.

Her own employer becomes part of the investigation: **Adrienne Shaw**,
her supervisor and the person who shaped her career, is shown to have
suppressed Palinode-linked claim patterns for years. The confrontation
between them should be one of the book's hardest scenes — real
mentorship and real institutional betrayal, simultaneously true.

Irena Petrov's memory of "Anna" is confirmed as significant, not
mistaken: very likely Mara and Elena's own mother, processed through
Bellwether House decades earlier, fleeing danger connected to the girls'
father **(exact nature and father's fate: TBD Gate 3, deliberately left
open per `00_project/gate_1_concept_validation.md` Part B3)**. Mara
begins to understand that "Voss" is not her birth name and that her own
childhood, which she has always treated as the one fully verified fact
of her life, was itself constructed.

**Julian Vale** makes contact — not as a cornered antagonist but as
someone genuinely willing to talk, which is more unsettling than a
confrontation would be. He characterizes Palinode's history honestly
enough to be credible (its real protective successes alongside its real
harms) and reveals, in whatever specific way Gate 3 builds the scene,
that he regards Mara as Palinode's greatest success — the clearest
possible on-page demonstration of `02_novel_bible/palinode_system.md`'s
"identity is infrastructure, not essence" thesis, applied to Mara's own
life.

Mara also learns more of Elena's actual, complicated role: not purely a
victim investigating from outside, but a former Palinode
operative/contractor who understood the organization from the inside
before turning against it — carrying real complicity the book must not
soften once Elena reappears.

Part II closes on a major reversal/betrayal **(exact mechanism TBD, Gate
3 — candidates include: a piece of evidence Mara trusted turning out to
be Palinode-constructed; Adrienne or Teo Marchetti making a choice that
costs Mara access or safety; Julian Vale executing a move that puts
Elena or a witness in genuine danger)** that removes whatever safety
Mara believed she still had, at roughly the 60–70% structural mark.

## Part III — The First Tenant (Ch. 35–46, ~22,000–26,000 words)

Following the all-is-lost point (~75%), Mara locates Elena — alive, but
damaged by years of performed identity and by the specific cost of
turning against Palinode from inside it. Their reunion is not simple
relief; it has to carry the accumulated weight of the whole book's
"loved each other badly" argument, dramatized directly rather than
resolved by the fact of being reunited.

The climax (selected Ending Architecture 1, refined with Architecture
3's trust-based beat — full detail in `ending_options.md`) turns on
Mara's choice: total exposure, which would be simpler and more
"correct" by her own former standard, versus selective exposure, which
protects real, vulnerable people Palinode also sheltered. The single
highest-weighted decision inside the climax is Mara choosing to act on
Elena's judgment over her own fully verified evidence on one crucial
point — the book's thematic argument resolved through action, not just
realized through reflection.

Julian Vale's downfall is personal as well as institutional **(exact
mechanism TBD, Gate 4)** — Palinode's leadership, criminal-client work,
and financial infrastructure exposed; its legitimately protected
identities left standing through Mara's deliberate, careful choices.

Mara ends the book having used identity-construction logic herself —
transparently, consensually, once — to help build Elena a genuine new
foundation, the ironic and intentional completion of her arc. The
sisters reach a real, specific, imperfect reconciliation: not
everything is repaired, but the pattern (proof demanded instead of
belief offered) is named and, at least once, broken. Final image: TBD
Gate 4, working candidate is a deliberate, chosen echo of the book's
opening paradox — Mara now knowingly holding an incomplete or altered
record of her own life, this time by her own honest choice rather than
someone else's concealment.
