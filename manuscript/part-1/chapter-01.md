# Chapter One — Move-in Day

The listing photographs hadn't shown the rain. Nora supposed that was the
one honest omission in an otherwise suspiciously accurate set of
pictures — she'd surveyed enough over-dressed flats to know when a
wide-angle lens was lying to her, and Sovereign Mill's hadn't been, which
should have reassured her more than it did.

She backed the car up to the kerb on Ropewalk Lane for the second time
that day, cut the engine, and sat a moment listening to the rain get
louder now the wipers had stopped fighting it. In the boot: a box of books
she'd never finished reading, four still-flattened boxes she'd bought and
not yet needed, and the lamp from her side of the bed, which she'd taken
partly because she liked it and partly because leaving it behind would
have felt, at two in the morning packing alone, like conceding a point
she hadn't agreed to argue.

Marcus had offered to help with this last run. She'd said she had it. She
did not, strictly, have it — the box of books alone was going to require a
strategy — but there was having it, and there was needing him to watch her
not have it, and she'd rather stand in Ropewalk Lane in the rain for the
rest of the afternoon than do the second one again.

She got the books out first, because they were the worst of it, and
straightened up under their weight to look at the building properly for
what was, in a real sense, the first time. She'd done the viewing in
October, in flat grey daylight, with a checklist running in her head she
hadn't been able to switch off. Today she let herself just look.

Sovereign Mill stood back from the lane behind its own strip of setts,
five storeys of dark brick gone the particular soot-black that only a
century and a half in a working city could produce, softened at the
roofline by a run of tall mill windows and, over what had clearly once
been a loading bay, a timber-and-iron beam that jutted out above the door
as though the building were still deciding whether to hoist something up.
Somebody had chosen to leave it there rather than take it down, and to
light it gently from below once the afternoon went dark, and the effect,
if you liked that sort of thing, which against her better professional
judgement she rather did, was of a building that knew exactly how good it
looked and had simply decided not to make a fuss about it.

The box was heavier than the rain made it feel fair for it to be. She got
it as far as the door before her arms made clear, in the specific
language arms used, that this was as far as they intended to go without
help, and the door — heavy, black-painted steel dressed up to look older
than the hinges suggested it was — opened before she'd worked out how to
knock with her hands full.

"You'll want to put that down before it puts you down," said the man
holding it open. Sixties, heavyset, a cardigan on despite what she could
already feel was an aggressively heated lobby. "Desmond Pye. I do the
building."

"Nora Hale. Flat Six." She said it and felt the mild vertigo of it being
true.

"I know which one you are." He said it without any weight on it at all,
nodding her through into the lobby, and she filed the sentence away the
way she filed most things lately — as either completely unremarkable or
the first data point of something, with no way yet to tell which.

The lobby did the thing the photographs had done: undersold itself by
being exactly as advertised. The old hoist beam continued through from
outside, spanning the full width of the ceiling, iron fittings picked out
in a black that was either original grime sealed under lacquer or a very
good facsimile of it; the floor was reclaimed board; the carpet on the
stairs beyond, glimpsed through a propped fire door, was a bottle-green
dark enough to read as almost black until the light caught it. Post boxes
and a buzzer panel took up one wall, eleven flats listed and, at the top,
a plate that said only THE LOFT, no number, which told her something about
the building's internal politics before she'd met a single other
resident.

She found FLAT 6 near the bottom. Someone had printed HALE on a strip of
white label tape and stuck it over another label, badly — its edges
didn't quite reach, and underneath, in a slightly different, older
typeface, she could still read the ghost of it. FERRIS. The old tape's
residue had gone grey-brown at the margins where the new label failed to
cover it, like a scar healing under a plaster put on a size too small.

"Previous tenant," Pye said, following her look, the way you'd confirm
the time if someone glanced at a clock. "We'll get you a proper one made
up. I keep meaning to."

"No rush." She meant it. It would be months, if she was honest with
herself, before she stopped being surprised to see her own married name on
anything at all, let alone minded whose label it had replaced.

She was still working out how to manage the box, the door, and the lift
she could now see tucked behind the stairs — an actual cage, iron lattice
concertina gate, plainly the building's original goods hoist wearing a
fresh coat of paint and, presumably, a modern motor — when a second man
came down the last few stairs at a pace that suggested he'd heard the
front door and come to make sure of something.

"You must be Nora." He had her name in his mouth before she'd said it to
Pye loud enough for it to have carried, and a coat that had been cut for
him rather than bought off a rail, and a specific, focused warmth she
clocked immediately and couldn't help reading as the manner of a man
who'd worked out, somewhere along the way, that eye contact was
underused. "Julian Cole. I own the appalling privilege of this place." He
took the box before she'd agreed to let him, which was either good manners
or a mild power move, and she couldn't immediately decide which, and
disliked that she couldn't. "Saturday, you said. I told Desmond to expect
you at three and here you are at three. I like that in a tenant."

"I like being on time in general," she said, which was true, and easier
to say than anything about why the box currently in Julian Cole's arms
held eleven years of a marriage's paperback half of a shared bookshelf,
sorted, God help her, by who'd originally bought it. "You didn't have to
come down."

"I like meeting people properly. Landlords who don't are wasting the only
interesting part of the job." He was already moving toward the hoist,
unbothered by its size or hers. "You're at Hollis and Vane, Desmond tells
me. Chartered surveyor."

"It was on the application."

"It was." He smiled in a way that made the correction land as a
compliment rather than a catch, held the concertina gate for her with the
box still braced against one hip, and said, as the hoist began its slow,
deliberate clank upward, close enough to a joke that she let herself
half-laugh, "I'll try not to take it personally, having a professional in
the building. You'll all find out eventually what we actually spent the
budget on."

The flat, when Cole finally let her into it — insisting, cheerfully, on
checking the radiators were bled and the smoke alarm was live while she
stood in her own new hallway feeling like a guest in it — smelled of
fresh paint in a way the tenancy start date, three days ago on paper,
didn't quite account for. Under that, the flatter, colder smell of a room
that had been properly, professionally cleaned rather than simply tidied:
no dust in the window tracks, no smear on the single glazing's inner pane,
the kind of finish she recognised from handover surveys on new-builds, not
from a rental that had, according to the file, last changed hands eight
months before.

"You'll let us know if anything's not right," Cole said, setting her box
down in the sitting room with a care that suggested he'd carried worse.
"Desmond's in the building most hours. I'm effectively always in the
building, which I'm told is either reassuring or the opposite depending
who you ask." He said it lightly, watching her a beat too long for it to
be entirely a joke, and then he was gone, and Pye with him, and the front
door — a good door, she noted despite herself, proper intumescent strip,
self-closer set correctly, not one of the compromises a lifetime in the
trade had taught her to expect — clicked shut on its own weight, and she
was alone in it for the first time.

She stood in the middle of the sitting room and let herself have thirty
seconds of simply feeling what it was: hers, for now, in the specific,
limited way a tenancy agreement made a thing yours. A cast iron column
stood where an architect had clearly fought to keep it rather than box it
in, black-painted, cool under her palm when she touched it, the kind of
detail the brochure had photographed from exactly the right angle. The
mill window behind it was vast, single-glazed behind a slim secondary
unit that had already misted very faintly at one corner — first sign of
the cold snap the forecast had been threatening all week.

On the kitchen worktop, someone — Pye, presumably, ahead of her arrival —
had left a short stack of post rubber-banded together. Three items were
addressed to N. Hale, forwarded from the solicitor's box she'd been using
as a stopgap address for a month. The fourth, on the bottom, was addressed
to C. Ferris.

She turned it over. No return address. A window envelope, the kind banks
and utility companies used, nothing that told her anything except that
somebody's records hadn't caught up with a change of tenant eight months
old, which was either sloppy or simply what happened to post in general,
the world over, always. She set it on the counter to hand back to Pye and
didn't think about it again for as long as it took to bring four more
boxes up in the hoist, alone this time, listening to it clank at a pace
that made her wonder whether slow was a design choice or just old age.

It was on the third trip, manoeuvring a dismantled bookcase through the
door sideways, that she caught the wall.

She hadn't been looking for it — she wasn't working, this wasn't a job,
she kept telling herself that — but twelve years in the trade had put
something in her hands and eyes that didn't clock off simply because the
ceiling over her head was, for the next twelve months at least,
technically her own problem to look after rather than someone else's to
certify. The plaster on the party wall, the one she'd already decided
would take her desk, was a shade paler than the wall around it, a
rectangle perhaps two feet by three at roughly shoulder height, the join
not quite flush under a raking light. A patch. Recent-ish — the paint
still had that faint chalky new-emulsion smell she'd clocked at the front
door, concentrated here, at its source.

Not unusual, on its own. Buildings got patched. Pipework moved, sockets
relocated, a snagging list worked through before a new tenant arrived. She
ran a thumb along the join anyway, the way she would on a site she was
being paid to distrust, and found nothing underneath it that told her a
story — no damp, no give, just a slightly amateur finishing hand on an
otherwise very professionally finished building, one small human
inconsistency in a flat that had, up to this point, been suspiciously good
at hiding its seams.

She filed it away rather than flagging it — that was how she handled
most things these days — and went back to the bookcase.

Her phone buzzed on the windowsill somewhere around eight, while she was
on her knees breaking down the last of the boxes. *Let me know you got in
okay*, it said, and then, a minute later, unable to leave it there, *Also
the house completion stuff — no rush, whenever*. She looked at it long
enough for the screen to dim on its own, thumb not quite committing to the
reply, and then turned it face-down on the sill and went back to the
boxes. There would be a version of tonight where she answered that
properly, said something generous, meant it even. Tonight was not going
to be that version.

By ten she had done the parts of unpacking that actually mattered — bed
made, kettle findable, the box of books abandoned against the wall in a
state that could generously be called shelved — and was back at the party
wall, on her knees this time for a duller reason, coaxing the bookcase's
base flush against a skirting board that wasn't, it turned out, quite as
flush as the rest of the room.

The skirting ran proud along a four-foot stretch directly beneath the
patch, a good half-centimetre of it standing off the wall where somebody
had reinstated it without re-scribing the cut — another small human
failure, in the one part of the flat nobody had thought to photograph. She
got a hand behind it to press it back, more from the same reflex that had
made her check the fire door than any real plan to fix it tonight, and
felt paper.

Not a mouse's nest, not stray insulation. A single scrap, dry and a little
furred at the edge the way paper went when it had sat undisturbed a long
time, small enough that she had to work two fingers in after it before it
came free.

It was the corner of a page, torn — not cut — from something spiral-bound;
she could see the pale, dog-eared crescent of a punched hole at the top,
the kind a reporter's flip pad took. Someone had written on it in blue
biro, in a fast, private hand that hadn't been meant for anyone else to
read, and the tear had taken most of whatever the sentence had been and
left her four words of it, and a fragment of a fifth.

*—ask D re: 4th flr rep—*

She sat back on her heels and read it twice, the second time specifically
to talk herself out of the first.

It meant nothing. It was the torn corner of somebody else's to-do list,
older than the tenancy file made this flat's history sound, missed by
cleaners who had otherwise done a flawless job — exactly the kind of thing
that turned up behind skirting
boards in every building she'd ever surveyed, in the handwriting of a
person who no longer lived here and had, as far as anyone in this
building seemed concerned, simply gone.

She should put it in the bin.

She got up instead, found an evidence bag still sitting in the bottom of
her site kit from a job in September, and stood there a moment feeling
faintly ridiculous, holding a torn scrap of someone else's handwriting in
a home she had told herself, three hours ago, she intended to stop
treating like a site.

In it went anyway, and the bag went in the drawer of the bedside table on
what had already, without her quite deciding it, become her side of a bed
that was for once entirely her own. She turned off the lamp she'd fought a
marriage's worth of guilt to bring with her, and lay for a long time
listening to a building she did not yet know well enough to recognise the
ordinary sounds of — trying to work out which of them were ordinary at
all.
