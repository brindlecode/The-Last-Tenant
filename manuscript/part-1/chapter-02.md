# Chapter Two — Settling In

By Monday she'd stopped pretending she wasn't doing it on purpose. The
fire door onto the second-floor landing had a self-closer fitted to it, a
good one, and every morning on her way to the lift — a small brass plate
screwed to the lattice gate read THE HOIST, capitalised, entirely in
earnest, and she'd decided she liked the building enough to play along —
she let the door swing shut behind her instead of holding it, and watched
to see how far it travelled before the latch caught. Full swing, clean
catch, every time. It told her nothing she needed to know. She did it
anyway, the way some people checked a stove was off from the car.

It wasn't only the door. By Tuesday she'd caught herself counting exits
in the lobby without deciding to — two, the front door and the fire exit
through the old loading bay, one more than building regulations strictly
required for a residential conversion this size, which told her more
than anything in the brochure had that whoever drew up the original
planning submission had wanted the sign-off to be easy rather than merely
adequate. By Wednesday she'd read every extinguisher's last-service tag
in the ground-floor corridor while waiting for a parcel she could have
collected in half the time if she hadn't stopped to do it. None of it was
for anyone. It was simply what her eyes did now, unsupervised, the way
someone else's might drift to a shop window.

She found the Ropewalk Café the same unsupervised way, on the walk back
from that parcel: a converted weighing-house at the end of the lane, a
bell over the door, a barista called Sam who didn't ask how she took her
coffee twice after the first time. She started going most mornings ahead
of the Hoist, partly because the coffee in the flat was still in a box
somewhere and partly because it was ten minutes of the day that belonged
to absolutely no one else — a small, portable, entirely legal kind of
leaving.

She was midway through Thursday's version of the door ritual when the door
opposite hers opened and a woman about her own age leaned out holding a
paintbrush like a cigarette, nowhere near any paint that Nora could see.

"You're testing the door," the woman said, delighted, not accusing.

"I'm testing the door."

"Cass used to do that. I always thought it was an OCD thing. Is it a job
thing?"

"It's a job thing that looks like an OCD thing." Nora let the door go one
more time, pointlessly, now that she had an audience. "Nora. Six."

"Rena. Five, obviously." She had paint on the heel of one hand,
cadmium-orange gone brown at the edges, and was wearing an oversized grey
jumper with a moth hole under one arm that Nora would have taken for
simply hers if Rena hadn't caught her looking and said, cheerfully
unbothered, "It's not mine. It's Cass's. She left it and I never got round
to — well." A small shrug, not quite finishing the sentence: the first
person in the building to say the name out loud to Nora without being
asked first.

"D'you want a coffee? I've got about forty minutes before I have to
pretend I've been working all morning."

Nora had ten before she needed to leave for a site visit into the city
and took the coffee anyway, standing in Rena's doorway while Rena made it
without quite inviting her over the threshold, which Nora respected more
than she'd have respected being asked in.

"So Cass," she said, aiming for idle. "She just left?"

"Honestly, I don't really know. One week she's normal — bit stressed,
moaning about work same as anyone — and then she's texting that she's got
a change of plans and needs to get her head straight somewhere, could I
keep an eye on a couple of boxes, and then—" Rena spread her hands, coffee
sloshing. "Nothing. I texted a few times. Read, no reply, and then not
even read. Told myself she'd blocked me, which, fine, people do that, we
weren't that close. But she never came back for the boxes. Cole had them
cleared with the rest of it eventually. I kept the jumper because I was
wearing it the day she left and it felt stupid to hand back a jumper and
nothing else, and now it feels stupid to bring up at all, four seasons
later, still wearing it."

"What kind of change of plans?"

"No idea. She mentioned some work thing, once — some stupid thing about
the stairs, she called it. I thought she meant office politics, the
metaphorical kind." Rena laughed, a little uncertainly, like she was
hearing herself say it for the first time and not liking it as much as
she had the first time she'd told the story. "Didn't ask more. You don't,
with a work thing. Everyone's got one."

"D'you like it here?" Nora asked, mostly to move them both off it.
"Properly, I mean."

"Love it. I know I'm meant to have opinions about corporate landlords
ruining the character of the neighbourhood, and Julian's a lot, but he
fixed my radiator on a Sunday once without being asked twice, and I can
leave whenever I want without six months of admin, and I get to paint in
the kind of light you'd pay double for somewhere with a garden." Rena
drained her coffee. "I'm not staying forever. I just like not having
decided that yet."

Nora thought about the stairs line on and off for the rest of the day,
the way you worry at a word that's started to look misspelled the longer
you stare at it.

* * *

Marcus called on Friday, which was itself a kind of information, since
he'd taken to texting first lately and waiting to be told a call was
allowed.

"I need you to sign the completion paperwork by Monday or we lose the
buyer's slot," he said, without preamble, in the specific voice he used
when he'd rehearsed sounding reasonable. "I can drop it round. Or email,
whichever's easier."

"Email's easier."

"Right. Email." A pause that cost him something. "How's the new place?"

"It's fine."

"Nora."

"It's fine, Marcus. It's a flat. I've put my books on a shelf." She was
standing at the mill window when she said it, watching Ropewalk Lane go
dark at half four, and didn't examine too closely why she'd chosen to
make it sound smaller than it was — except that the honest alternative
was telling him about a torn scrap of somebody else's handwriting sitting
in her bedside drawer, and there was no version of that conversation that
ended anywhere she wanted to be.

"I just want this to be easy," he said. "For both of us."

It was, she thought, almost exactly the sentence he'd used the week he'd
arranged the remortgage — back when he still believed he could manage the
outcome quietly enough that she'd never need to know there'd been a risk
at all. She was fairly sure he had no memory of having said it then, which
was somehow worse than if he had.

"I'll email you tonight," she said, and did, and didn't mention the flat
again.

She stood at the window a while after she'd hung up, phone warm in her
hand, watching a couple walk a dog she recognised from the Ropewalk in
the wrong direction for this time of evening. Eleven years, and he could
still say four ordinary words and put her straight back in the kitchen at
Aldermoor Road, reading a letter that hadn't been addressed to her, in
the exact position her whole marriage had apparently been arranging for
her to stand in without telling her. She didn't cry. She'd used most of
that up over the last year and had little left to spend, and what
remained ran along drier, more useful channels now — the kind that made
her check a fire door twice, or read a stranger's forwarded post twice, or
stand at a window a while longer than the view deserved before she let
herself stop.

* * *

Saturday she did laundry, which meant discovering the first-floor laundry
room existed, by the process of elimination that came from failing to
find one on her own, and meeting no one, and standing for forty minutes in
a small warm room that smelled of communal fabric softener, reading the
noticeboard for want of anything else to read: a lost key, a book club
that met on Tuesdays she had already decided she wasn't ready for, a
laminated notice about recycling days, and, pinned slightly apart from
the rest as though somebody had put it up and then had second thoughts
about how prominent to make it, a Sovereign Living notice about "essential
works to the fourth-floor plant room, access temporarily restricted, we
apologise for any inconvenience" — dated the previous winter, and never
taken down.

With the washing turning behind its little porthole, she gave in to the
kind of curiosity she'd have called perfectly professional if anyone had
asked, and took the Hoist all the way up.

The roof terrace was exactly what the brochure had promised and mostly
empty in November: raised beds gone to bare stalks, a pizza oven shrouded
under a tarpaulin, a view west over the wharf toward the working
scrapyard the residents' committee — if the building had ever managed to
form one — would presumably spend years trying to get rid of. Rena's flat
had almost the same view, she'd learn later, half a floor down and to the
left. She stood at the parapet a while, cold enough to be honest about
it, and let the Hoist's motor clank somewhere below her, ferrying someone
else up or down, before she gave up on the view and took the stairs back
rather than wait for it.

The stair was the building's original one, she was fairly sure — stone
treads worn very slightly concave in the centre of each, the kind of wear
that took a hundred years of feet, boxed into its own enclosure sometime
during the conversion and finished in the same bottle-green carpet as
everywhere else, though here it ran over stone rather than over a joist
floor, and her own footsteps barely reached her ears over her breathing.
Whoever had built the box around it had done a competent, unremarkable
job. It was, she thought, without particularly meaning anything by it,
the quietest part of a very well-appointed building, in a way the
Hoist — which she could still hear, two floors down now, still climbing
for whoever had called it — simply wasn't built to be.

The fourth-floor landing was where the stair let her back out into
carpet, and where, beside a door she hadn't clocked from the Hoist side
of the building, a steel door sat flush in the brickwork under a small
sign — PLANT ROOM. AUTHORISED ACCESS ONLY — fronted by a scuffed arc on
the carpet, the nap crushed and greyed in a fan shape a door only left
behind if it was opened often, wide, by people in a hurry or carrying
something. Not the mark of a room a caretaker let himself into once a
quarter with a torch and a clipboard. She looked at it the way she looked
at wear patterns on any commercial stair tread, automatically translating
it into footfall, frequency, month, and then made herself stop, because
there was no earthly reason for her to be running quantity-surveyor
arithmetic on a landing that had nothing to do with her tenancy, in a
building she had lived in for eight days.

The notice downstairs had said *temporarily*. She did the arithmetic
without meaning to anyway — the previous winter to this one was the
better part of a year, an odd length of time for *temporary* to still be
doing all the work in that sentence — and then made herself stop that
too.

She went home and made dinner and told herself that was the end of it,
and mostly believed herself.

* * *

The flat came with a tablet screwed to the wall by the front door,
Sovereign Living's idea of a concierge: a home screen for maintenance
requests, parcel notifications, the community noticeboard she'd already
met a paper version of downstairs, a running balance for anyone paying
rent by standing order. She'd meant all week to set up her own login and
kept not getting round to it, and that evening, keys still in her hand,
she finally tapped it awake to do exactly that.

It was already awake. Already logged in. Not to a blank setup screen but
to somebody's actual account, mid-session, a small red badge sitting over
a message icon like it had been sitting there patiently for months,
waiting for someone to come back and deal with it.

C FERRIS, the header said, pleasantly. WELCOME BACK.

She should have logged it out there. She told herself afterwards that
she'd meant to — that her thumb had gone toward the icon to do exactly
that, sign out, start clean, and that the notification had simply opened
on its own the way notifications did, and that much was true. It was also
true that she read it.

*J.C. — re: the report — can we talk before you—*

The rest was cut off by the preview window's own limits, no way to see
more without opening the message properly, going into an inbox that had
never once been hers to open. She stood with her thumb over it for longer
than she'd ever admit to, in a hallway that still smelled, very faintly,
of paint that should have finished drying weeks ago.

Then she backed out, found the account settings, and logged Cass Ferris
out of her own front door for the last time, and did not go looking for a
way back in.

She told herself that counted for something, and went to bed early, and
lay listening again for the building's ordinary sounds. Except she had a
few more of them now than she'd had a week ago, and one new question she
hadn't had before either: what a person called Cass Ferris had needed to
say to Julian Cole, urgently enough to still be sitting there logged in
and waiting, eight months after she'd apparently stopped needing anything
in this building at all.
