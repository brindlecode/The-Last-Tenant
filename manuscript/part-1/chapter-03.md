# Chapter Three — The Dossier Thread

She had a genuine reason to be at her desk on a Tuesday lunchtime few of
her colleagues used for anything but sandwiches: a backlog of report
admin she'd been behind on for a fortnight, exactly the kind of chore
that made pulling up the council's planning portal look like the least
interesting thing anyone could possibly be doing with a lunch hour.
Sovereign Mill's file was three clicks from a search she told herself was
reflexive — you checked a building's planning history the way you checked
its EPC rating, out of habit, nothing to do with anything in particular —
and then she was in it, rain starting up again against the office
windows, the first they'd had since the weekend.

The building control file was thinner than she'd have expected for a
change of use this size, and what was there told a story she recognised
more than she liked: initial notice lodged with the council, then —
partway through the works, a switch she'd seen often enough to never
think twice about — completion handled instead by a private approved
inspector, a firm called Rookford Building Control Partnership she'd never
once crossed paths with in twelve years of practice. Which didn't mean
anything on its own. Developers moved from council building control to
private inspectors constantly, usually for speed, sometimes for exactly
the reason everyone assumed and almost nobody ever proved. She noted the
firm's name anyway, the way she noted everything.

The planning file, three tabs over, was thicker, and mostly boring in the
specific way planning files were always mostly boring: drainage
conditions, a bat survey, six months of argument about a bin store nobody
currently used correctly regardless. She was skimming past the public
comments — nobody read the public comments, they were where planning
departments sent objections to be technically acknowledged and
effectively filed — when a name stopped her scrolling thumb dead against
the trackpad.

*Objection — C. Ferris (Rookford Courier, freelance) — object to condition
14 (fire strategy) on grounds that phased sign-off of means-of-escape
works creates unacceptable risk during the construction period; request
full council inspection prior to occupation.*

Dated three years before Nora had ever heard the words Sovereign Mill.
Long before, as far as she could tell from the flat's own paperwork, Cass
Ferris had ever lived there at all.

She read it four times. Then she sat back in a chair, in an open-plan
office that suddenly felt very loud, and thought: she didn't move in
because of a nice flat and a good deal. She moved in because of this.

On instinct more than intention, she typed Julian Cole's name into the
same portal and watched four other applications resolve out of the search
bar — three completed conversions across Rookford over the better part of
a decade, one still live. She didn't open any of them. There was a
version of this lunch hour that turned into an afternoon, and an
afternoon that turned into something she'd eventually have to explain to
someone, and she wasn't ready to find out which of those versions she was
currently living in. She closed the tab, finished the report she was
actually paid to be writing, and went home in weather that had turned,
sometime while she wasn't looking, back to rain.

* * *

There was more mail waiting when she got home that evening, wet at one
corner where the porch roof didn't quite cover the post table, and on top
of the usual two for N. Hale sat a stiff white envelope addressed to C.
Ferris with a return address printed along the flap in small grey
capitals: ROOKFORD BUILDING CONTROL PARTNERSHIP.

She stood in the lobby holding it for long enough that Pye, passing
through with a bag of bulbs he was apparently taking to be planted
somewhere, said, not unkindly, "That'll be more of the same, I expect.
Never really stops for some of them."

"More of the same as what?" she asked, keeping her voice as light as his.

He considered the bulbs in his bag as if they might answer for him.
"Post. Companies. They take their time catching up, some of them, longer
than you'd credit." A beat, his eyes somewhere over her shoulder now. "She
had a lot of post, near the end. More than most." And then, visibly
hearing himself, he hitched the bag higher on his hip and said something
about the courtyard beds needing turning before the frost got in, and was
gone before she could ask what *near the end* was supposed to mean.

"Right," she said to the space where he'd been, and carried the envelope
upstairs with the rest, and put it on the kitchen counter rather than in
the bin,
face down, the way you'd put down something that had turned out to be
heavier than it looked.

She didn't open it. It wasn't hers to open, and she was fairly sure that
particular line was one she still meant to hold, even now. But she
looked at the return address a long time before she went to bed, turning
the same three words over the way she'd been turning the file over all
afternoon, and thought that a coincidence was a very small container for
what she'd just found.

* * *

"So," Priya said, two coffees deep at the Ropewalk, with the particular
stillness she got when she'd already decided this wasn't going to be a
normal catch-up. "Tell me about the building."

Nora told her some of it. The objection, the fast-tracked sign-off, the
tenant who'd left without a forwarding address, the landlord who
personally welcomed people to a flat he apparently also personally
cleared out — all of it true, all of it the kind of thing she could say
out loud without having to explain why she had a torn corner of somebody
else's notebook in her bedside drawer, or why she'd once stood with her
thumb over another woman's private messages for longer than she was
proud of.

Priya listened with her lawyer face on, which Nora had always found more
use than the sympathetic one. "And you're looking into this because—"

"Because it's a twenty-minute public records search and I was curious."

"Right." Priya turned her cup a quarter turn without drinking from it.
"Nor. When the Marcus thing happened, you went through eleven months of
joint statements, line by line. Every coffee, every everything. You said
it was due diligence."

"It was due diligence."

"It was you needing every version of a story to make sense before you'd
let yourself feel anything about it. Which — fine, that's a way to
survive a thing, I'm not being funny about it. I'm asking if this is the
same shape."

"It's a genuine irregularity," Nora said. "Fast-tracked sign-off, private
inspector, an objection nobody ever followed up on. That's not me
inventing a pattern out of nothing."

"I believe you. I'm a solicitor, not an idiot — if you're telling me the
paperwork's odd, the paperwork's odd." Priya softened, which she did
rarely enough that it always landed. "I just want it to be the only thing
you're doing with your evenings. Not the thing you're doing instead of
unpacking a marriage."

"I've unpacked the boxes."

"That's not what I said."

Nora turned her own cup, considering it properly instead of just being
annoyed by the question, which was new behaviour for her and she noted it
with faint surprise. "Maybe. Probably, a bit. That doesn't mean I'm wrong
about the file."

"No," Priya agreed, "it doesn't. That's what makes it an annoying
question and not actually an answer." She reached over and stole the last
of Nora's flapjack without asking, which was as close as Priya came to a
gesture of affection in public. "Are you eating properly, by the way. Not
just what's in the flat."

"I'm eating."

"That wasn't a yes."

Nora let her have that one, because it wasn't, and because the alternative
was telling her about the envelope currently sitting face-down on her
kitchen counter like something asleep, and she still didn't have the
first idea how to say that sentence out loud to someone who loved her
enough to make her stop.

They hugged goodbye on the cobbles outside, longer than usual, and Priya
held her a moment at arm's length afterwards, doing the appraising thing
she'd been doing since university, the one that always made Nora feel
simultaneously five years old and entirely seen. "You'd tell me," Priya
said, "if it stopped being a five-minute records search."

"I'd tell you."

It wasn't quite a lie. She simply hadn't decided yet when *stopped* was
going to turn out to have happened.

* * *

There was a note waiting when she got home two days later, slid under
the door rather than left with the rest of the post, the handwriting
confident enough that she suspected he'd had practice being complimented
on it. *Nora — a few of us do dinner now and then, nothing formal. This
Friday, 7, mine, if you're free. Bring nothing but yourself. J.*

She thought about saying no. She said yes by text within the hour, and
spent more time than she wanted to admit deciding what a person wore to
dinner with her landlord, before settling on exactly what she'd have worn
anywhere else, on the theory that changing anything about herself for
Julian Cole was a door she didn't want to learn she could open.

She took the Hoist up rather than the stairs, on the grounds that arriving
out of breath at a stranger's dinner party was its own kind of admission,
and stood a moment on the landing outside the Loft's single, unnumbered
door, listening to laughter already going on the other side of it, before
she made herself knock.

The Loft turned out to be less a flat than a single held breath of a
room, the whole top floor knocked through except for a bedroom suite
behind glazed doors at one end, every one of the mill's original roof
trusses left exposed and uplit so the ceiling looked like the ribcage of
something enormous that had once, a very long time ago, been a forest.
Rena was already there,
cross-legged on a windowsill with a glass of wine, delighted and entirely
unsurprised to see her; a couple from the third floor filled out the rest
of the table, deep in a running argument about whether their kitchen
extension would ever actually start on the date they'd been promised.
Outside the enormous mill windows the night had gone hard and clear, the
first properly cold one since she'd moved in, Rookford's sodium lights
strung out along the wharf like something laid out for inspection.

Cole cooked, which she hadn't expected, and cooked well, which she'd
expected even less to find charming and did anyway, and for most of the
evening he was simply a good host — funny about the building's
renovation disasters, generous topping up glasses, entirely uninterested
in being the most important person at his own table, which was itself,
she thought, a remarkably effective way of being exactly that.

He told a story about the building's rewiring that had the third-floor
couple — Priti and Dev, she was fairly sure, or close enough that she
wasn't going to risk it out loud — properly laughing: something about a
Victorian junction box wired directly into a modern consumer unit by a
contractor who'd since, in Cole's telling, "gone to work exclusively on
listed churches, where I can only hope to God the stakes are lower."
Rena, who had clearly heard the story before, mouthed the punchline along
with him, fond rather than bored. Nora found herself laughing too,
genuinely, and filed the fact away with a faint, private unease: it was
entirely possible to enjoy an evening and distrust its host inside the
same three hours, and she wasn't yet sure which of those was supposed to
win.

It was gone ten, the third-floor couple deep in an argument about tile
grout, Rena refilling her own glass without asking, when Cole leaned over
to top up Nora's water and said, conversationally, like it cost him
nothing at all, "You'll have to forgive the flat still smelling of paint,
by the way. We were finishing off snagging when Cass left, and some
things got missed."

She hadn't told him the name. She was almost entirely sure of that — went
back over every conversation they'd had, twice, in the time it took him
to finish the sentence — and equally sure there were three or four
perfectly ordinary ways he could have it regardless: the tenancy file,
the buzzer label before Pye retaped it, Pye himself, mentioning a previous
tenant the way anyone mentions a previous tenant. Any one of them was
completely reasonable. All of them together were still just a list of
reasons, not an explanation she'd actually watched happen.

"Cass," she said, testing the name in her own mouth, watching him for
something she couldn't have named if asked. "Yes. She left it very clean,
actually."

"She was thorough." Something moved behind his face for exactly as long
as it took to refill a water glass, and not one second longer, and then
he was topping up the tile-grout couple's wine and asking Rena about a
commission, and the moment closed over so smoothly that if Nora hadn't
spent twelve years being paid to notice the difference between a wall
that had never cracked and one that had been patched and skimmed and
painted back to looking like it never had, she might have let the whole
thing go.

She didn't let it go. She simply didn't say so out loud.

She said goodnight properly, thanked him for dinner and meant it, because
it had genuinely been a good dinner, and that was possibly the most
unsettling part of the entire evening — that both things could be true in
the same breath, the food and the warmth and the good company entirely
real, and so was the small cold fact she carried down in the Hoist with
her afterwards, past the fourth floor, past a door she now thought about
every time she passed it whether she meant to or not.

She wasn't going to stop.

She let herself admit that much, alone, somewhere between the third floor
and the second, with the whole building clanking on around her, gently,
as if it already knew.
