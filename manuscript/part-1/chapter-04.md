# Chapter Four — The Fourth Floor

She woke early and lay still for a while, turning the dinner over in the
particular grey light that made everything that had felt significant the
night before look, briefly, like something she might have built out of
very little. A landlord had mentioned a former tenant's name. Landlords
did that. Her phone, when she finally reached for it, had nothing on it
but a good-morning from Priya and a forwarded email from Marcus's
solicitor about a completion date, and she typed *dinner was fine, tell
you properly at the weekend* to Priya and deleted it twice before sending
a version with less in it, because there wasn't yet a way to describe
what had actually happened at that table that didn't sound, even to her,
like the start of the thing Priya was worried she was doing.

She got up before she could argue herself all the way back round to
believing nothing was wrong, because some steadier part of her had
already noticed that the comfortable version of the story required her to
disbelieve a professional instinct that had never once, in twelve years,
been the thing that was lying to her.

The cold snap the forecast had been threatening since her first night in
the building had finally arrived properly overnight, hard and dry, the
courtyard's puddles gone rigid at the edges. She found Pye out in it
anyway, turning over one of the raised beds by the old loading bay with
the unhurried thoroughness of a man who'd decided the weather wasn't
worth arguing with. Bulbs again, or still — she couldn't tell if this was
a different job to the one she'd seen him at before or the same one
taking its time.

"You still owe me a label," she said, which was true, and easy, and got
her exactly as far into the conversation as she needed to be.

He almost smiled, not looking up from the bed. "I do. I'll do it this
week." The trowel kept moving, steady, unbothered. "You sleep alright?
Julian's dinners run long."

"Fine." She crouched near the edge of the bed, not quite helping, close
enough that the conversation could stay low without either of them
deciding to make it a private one out loud. "Desmond. Can I ask you
something that isn't about a label."

His hands didn't stop, but something in his shoulders did — a fractional
settling, the kind of stillness that arrived a half-second before someone
either lied to you very well or told you something they hadn't planned
to. "You can ask."

"Cass. Before she left — did she ever seem like something was actually
wrong? Not just work-stressed. Wrong."

For a while he didn't answer, and she let the silence do the work
instead of filling it, which was its own professional habit, the one
that got people to say the true thing instead of the rehearsed one.

"She asked me the same thing you're asking," he said, finally, still not
looking up. "Near enough word for word." He straightened, knees audibly
unhappy about it, and for a second his face did something she hadn't
seen it do before — not evasive, just old, and tired, and briefly
unguarded, a door left open by accident rather than choice. "About the
fourth floor. Wanted to know what used to be up there, before the
conversion. I told her what I tell everyone who asks, which isn't many.
Storage, mostly. Nothing worth knowing."

"And that was it? That's all she wanted?"

"That was it." He picked the trowel back up, and whatever had opened in
his face closed itself with the particular speed of a man who'd had
practice. "You want that label brass or plastic. Julian likes brass. I
think plastic's more honest about what it is." He was already moving
toward the shed, faster than the conversation strictly warranted, and she
let him go, because there was nothing else true left to ask him that
morning, and because she'd got, without quite meaning to, exactly the
thing she'd come down for.

* * *

She made herself wait an hour, on the theory that a plan you'd let sit
was a plan and not just a feeling, and used most of it failing to do
anything else. She got as far as opening her laptop and closing it again.
She thought about Priya's voice saying *the same shape* and decided, on
balance, that this was a different shape entirely — a shape with a
public planning objection in it, and a private inspector she'd never
heard of, and a caretaker who'd gone quiet and small in the space of one
sentence — and that the fact she could construct an argument for that
distinction so quickly wasn't proof either way, and that she was going up
regardless of which of them turned out to be right. Then she checked,
without pretending to herself she wasn't checking, that Pye's van was
still in the courtyard and his radio still on in the shed, and went up.

She took the stairs rather than the Hoist, for reasons she didn't examine
too closely, and was grateful for the first time for how little sound
they carried. The building was doing its ordinary Saturday things around
her — a door somewhere on the third floor, water in a pipe, the faint
bass of somebody's music leaking through a party wall — and none of it
had anything to do with her, and all of it made her feel, absurdly, more
conspicuous rather than less.

The fourth-floor landing was exactly as she remembered it from the
laundry-room detour weeks before: the Plant Room's steel door, the
scuffed carpet in front of it. New, since then, or new since she'd last
had reason to look — a length of hazard-striped tape stuck loosely across
the frame of Flat 10's door, the kind builders used to mark a threshold
rather than seal it. The door itself stood open six inches on an
unfastened chain, propped with a folded dust sheet against the draught.
A radio played somewhere inside, tinny, turned low. Paint fumes came
through the gap, sharp enough to sting at the back of her throat.

"Hello?" She said it to the gap, ready to be a concerned neighbour asking
about the smell, a story she'd built in the stairwell and was fairly
pleased with. Nobody answered. The radio kept playing to what was, as far
as she could tell, an empty room, and somewhere further off, a van door
slammed — the contractor, she guessed, fetching something, gone for as
long as a fetching took and not a minute longer.

She should have left it there. She knew that with total clarity, the same
clarity she'd have brought to any other line in a report she knew would
get her sued if she got it wrong, and she put two fingers against the
door and eased it another foot open anyway, just far enough to see in
without crossing the threshold, telling herself that was a real
distinction and not simply the one she currently needed it to be.

The room beyond was a stalled site in the specific way she'd seen a
hundred times professionally: dust sheets gone grey at the edges, a
stepladder nobody had folded away, a stack of plasterboard offcuts
leaning in the corner, two rubble bags slumped against the skirting
waiting for a skip that plainly hadn't come in some time. Everything wore
the particular staleness of a job that had genuinely, unglamorously
stopped — the dust on the sheets undisturbed, the paint tins along the
skirting sealed and unstirred, nothing about it staged for anyone's
benefit. That, more than anything, was what she believed instantly and
without deciding to: nobody had built this room to be found. It had
simply been left, the way rooms got left, mid-thought, for reasons nobody
had bothered writing down. But one section of the far wall was wrong in a
way the rest of the room wasn't: new plasterboard, taped and skimmed and
painted a white that hadn't had time to yellow the way the room's older
paint had, a strip of tape still holding along one edge where whoever
had finished it hadn't come back to peel it off. It was the only
complete thing in the room. Everything else looked like a job somebody
had walked away from and never quite returned to, on purpose, for eight
months, the way a story gets left exactly true enough not to need
finishing.

She counted the offcuts on pure reflex. She read the wall the way she
read every wall now, awake or not. And on top of the nearer rubble
bag, half sunk into a nest of dust sheet and broken batten as if it had
simply been set down in passing and never picked back up, sat a
spiral-bound notebook, its cover the exact burnt orange of a thing she'd
already met the torn corner of.

She didn't have to open it to know. She crossed the threshold anyway —
the distinction she'd made a minute ago dissolving the moment it stopped
being useful to her — crouched, and turned it over.

*C.F.*, in silver paint pen, hand-lettered, slightly uneven on the second
letter the way handwriting got when somebody did it on a knee instead of
a desk. She held the torn corner from her bedside drawer up against the
notebook's back page without needing to. It fit anyway. The fibres lined
up along the tear like they'd never really been apart, like the eight
months in between had happened to everything in the room except this one
small, exact, ordinary fact.

Her thumb found the edge of the cover, ready to open it, and she made
herself stop before it had travelled an inch. Not here. Not with a
contractor one flight of stairs away and a radio covering nothing but its
own noise, and not — some more honest admission, arriving a half-second
behind the practical one — without deciding first whether she was still
the kind of person who read what wasn't offered to her, or whether she'd
already stopped being able to answer that and simply hadn't said so out
loud yet. Taking it was already more than she'd let herself do with the
portal message. Reading it here, now, standing in somebody else's
wrecked, half-finished room, was a second line, and she was not, not yet,
going to cross both in the same afternoon.

Somewhere below her, through the floor, the Hoist's motor started up.

She didn't remember deciding to take it. She simply had it, inside her
coat, her pulse going hard enough that she could feel it in her teeth,
and she was back out through the gap in the door and easing it to behind
her — not quite shut, the way she'd found it, a detail she registered
being proud of even mid-panic, some small competent part of her still
filing evidence correctly while the rest of her came apart — when a
man's voice, close, unhurried, said something below her on the stair
that she didn't catch the words of, only the fact of it: someone coming
up, not down, taking the stairs rather than waiting for the Hoist that
was still climbing somewhere behind its own noise.

There was nowhere to be that wasn't this landing. She went for the only
door that wasn't Flat 10's or the Plant Room's — the fire door onto the
third-floor corridor one half-flight below, which opened, mercifully,
without a sound, some tenant's self-closer as well-specified as her own —
and stood on the wrong side of it with her back against cold paint,
counting footsteps she couldn't see, a stranger's unbothered half of a
phone call passing close enough on the other side of the door that she
could have named the football score he was disagreeing with. The
footsteps didn't slow. They carried on past her and up, unhurried,
unremarkable, a man going back to a job he had no idea anyone had just
been standing inside of, and a minute later a door somewhere above her —
Flat 10's, she assumed, though by then she was already moving and not
inclined to stop and confirm it — closed with the ordinary, unweighted
sound of someone who suspected nothing at all. She let the fire door
close itself behind her — slowly, correctly, the self-closer earning its
certificate — and took the rest of the stairs down at something closer to
a normal pace than she'd have believed herself capable of a minute
earlier.

*Quiet ones*, some very calm part of her mind noted, from a long way off,
as if this were a detail worth admiring rather than surviving, and she
reached her own landing just as the Hoist's gate clattered open on the
floor above, too late for whoever it was to see anything but a closed
door that had every right to be closed.

She let herself into Flat Six and did something she hadn't done once
since moving in: turned the deadbolt, not just the latch, and stood with
her back against her own front door, notebook still gripped inside her
coat, listening to her own pulse and the building's ordinary sounds and
finding, for the first time since she'd arrived, that she couldn't
immediately tell which was which.

Whatever story explained a stalled renovation, a locked plant room, and a
woman's notebook left sitting in a rubble bag eight months after she'd
supposedly packed up and gone in a hurry — it wasn't the story anyone in
this building had told her. She didn't know yet what had happened to
Cass Ferris. She was no longer able to pretend that not knowing was the
same thing as there being nothing to know.

She hadn't imagined any of it. That was the only thing she let herself be
certain of, standing there, coat still on, heart still going, a missing
woman's notebook held flat against her chest like something she'd caught
rather than taken.

*Dead* was the word that wanted to come next, and she made herself put it
back down, unproven, the way she'd have made a junior colleague strike an
unsupported conclusion from a report. *Missing.* That was as far as the
evidence currently let her go, and she was not, whatever else was
happening to her, going to be the one who stopped being careful about the
difference — not tonight, holding the one piece of proof in the entire
building that something in it had been lying to her from the day she
arrived.

She wasn't going back to the version of this where she'd imagined it.
That version was gone, and had, she suspected, never really been
available to her at all.

She thought, unbidden, of the letter that had told her about the
remortgage instead of the man she'd married telling her himself — of how
long she'd gone on believing the version of her own life that everyone
around her had apparently already stopped believing, simply because
nobody had thought her worth the trouble of the true one. She wasn't
going to be that particular kind of stupid again. Whatever this
building's true version of itself turned out to be, she intended to be
the one who found it, on purpose, with her eyes open, rather than the
last person in the room to be told.

Eventually she took her coat off. She did not, that night or for several
after, take the notebook out of the drawer where she put it, beside the
torn corner it now belonged to, both of them waiting for whatever came
next.
