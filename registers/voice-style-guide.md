# Voice & Style Guide

Living document. Amend only via a `DECISION_LOG.md` entry if a rule needs to
change after Chapter 1 sets precedent — otherwise treat as locked.

## POV & tense

- Close third person, past tense, single POV: **Nora Hale**, for the entire
  of Part I (and, per Gate 4's current plan, the whole novel).
- Free indirect discourse permitted and encouraged for her dry humor and
  self-correction — the narration can carry her sarcasm without dialogue
  tags or "she thought."
- No head-hopping. Other characters are known only through what Nora can
  see, hear, infer, or is told. This is the mechanical guarantee behind the
  prohibited-revelations system: if Nora can't know it, the prose can't
  state it.
- No prologue, no italicized villain-POV interludes. Cole's interiority is
  never accessed, in Part I or beyond.

## Mechanics

- British English spelling and usage throughout (colour, realise, grey,
  practise/practice per part of speech, plaster not sheetrock, flat not
  apartment, ground floor / 1st floor / 2nd floor... per UK convention as
  used in `bible/01-world-and-setting.md`).
- Double quotation marks for dialogue; single quotes only for a quote
  inside a quote.
- Serial (Oxford) comma.
- Numbers under 100 spelled out in narration except measurements,
  addresses, times, and money.
- Section breaks within a chapter: `* * *` centered on its own line (plain
  prose-safe glyph; avoids colliding with Markdown heading syntax).

## Diction boundaries

- Dread is built through precise, professional noticing, not adjectives.
  Prefer "the self-closer had been wedged with a folded beer mat" over "the
  door felt wrong." The building is never called creepy, sinister, or
  similar by the narration — let the reader do that work.
- Avoid gothic-horror stock phrases (whispers, shadows that move, a chill
  that has nothing to do with the temperature). If a sentence could appear
  unchanged in a ghost story, cut or ground it in something physical and
  specific instead.
- Nora's professional vocabulary is real and used correctly (surveying,
  building control, tenancy law) but never dumped in unbroken blocks —
  routed through what she's doing or noticing, not explained to the reader
  in lecture form.
- Interiority about the marriage stays economical. One precise image or
  exchange per scene beats a paragraph of rumination. Grief and anger are
  rationed, not because she doesn't feel them but because she's actively
  managing not to.

## Recurring motifs (use deliberately, don't overuse)

- **Noticing vs. saying**: Nora clocks things professionally and then
  chooses, consciously, whether to say them out loud. Track this choice —
  it's her character arc in miniature. Its literal vocabulary is
  **"filed [away]" vs. "flagged"** — used deliberately at the Ch.1 buzzer
  panel, the Ch.1 plaster patch, and the Ch.3 dinner — confirmed
  intentional during the Chapters 1–4 combined audit and documented here
  so it reads as motif rather than tic. Keep using this specific verb for
  this specific act; don't let it drift to other meanings.
- **Doors and closers**: fire doors, self-closers, propped doors, locked
  doors — the building's honesty or dishonesty is legible through how its
  doors behave. Recurs across Ch.1 (buzzer/lobby door), Ch.2 (fire door
  self-closers, Plant Room), Ch.4 (Flat 10's propped door, Nora's own door
  locked at the close).
- **Being managed vs. being told**: language of care that is actually
  control (Marcus's history; Cole's attentiveness) should echo without
  being pointed out by the narration — trust the reader.
- **The Hoist's clank** as an ambient, building-wide clock — use sparingly,
  as punctuation, not atmosphere filler.
- **"The building's ordinary sounds"**: a deliberate chapter-ending
  bookend, identified retroactively during the Chapters 1–4 combined
  audit and formalized here rather than left as an accident. Ch.1 closes
  with Nora unable to "recognise the ordinary sounds of" the building yet;
  Ch.2 closes "listening *again*... except she had a few more of them now
  than she'd had a week ago"; Ch.4 closes on the same listening act, "for
  the first time since she'd arrived," unable to tell her own pulse from
  the building's sounds. Each use must earn its place by measurably
  advancing what she now knows/fears relative to the last use — never
  repeat the beat without moving it forward.

## Chapter-opening/closing discipline

- Open chapters in scene, not in reflection or weather.
- Close chapters on an image or line with forward pressure — a question,
  an unresolved object, a choice made — never a summary of what just
  happened.

## Sentence-level craft checklist (used at the Prose Audit stage)

- Cut filtering words where they add distance without purpose (*she
  noticed*, *she felt*, *she saw*) unless the act of noticing is itself the
  point.
- Vary sentence length deliberately; avoid strings of same-length simple
  sentences (a common flat-affect trap when writing a controlled narrator).
- No two paragraphs in a row should open with the same subject/structure.
- Watch for era-generic thriller filler phrases ("little did she know,"
  "something told her," "a chill ran down her spine") — banned outright.
